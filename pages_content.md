## What makes you good teacher
Dr. Vikas Divyakirti discusses the qualities and teaching styles of a good teacher in today's era, especially concerning the "Gen Z" students. He highlights the challenges teachers face due to the reduced attention spans of students in the internet age (0:09) and the accessibility of information, which allows students to quickly verify facts (1:11).

To be an effective teacher today, he suggests focusing on two main aspects:

Interactive Communication: Teachers should aim for two-way communication, allowing students to be involved, ask questions, and receive answers (3:02).
Friendly Relationship with Students: Teachers need to understand that they are friends with the students, not dictators. Building a friendly relationship ensures students stay engaged even when making critical remarks (3:26).
Furthermore, he emphasizes two effective teaching methods:

Teaching through Stories and Examples: Incorporating good examples, stories, and poetic expressions to explain concepts (3:43).
Experiential Learning: Instead of just teaching about concepts, teachers should aim to provide experiences, such as showing relevant films or taking students to experience something firsthand, as direct observation leads to better understanding (4:02).

In this TEDx talk, Azul Terronez shares his 24-year obsession with the question: "What makes a good teacher great?" (0:21). He has collected 26,000 responses from students across diverse schools, from poor to elite, and found emerging patterns (0:25).

Terronez argues that schools are often afraid to truly listen to students (1:07). He explains that students have their own language for expressing their observations and desires (2:36). He provides examples:

When students say "great teachers eat apples," they mean teachers are willing to receive gifts and build trust (1:35, 2:15).
When students say a great teacher is "chill," they mean a teacher should be calm and not take things too seriously (2:40, 2:46).
He highlights key findings from his student responses:

Great teachers love to learn: While students see teachers teaching, they rarely see them learning (4:45). Students want teachers to be inspired by learning and to learn alongside them (5:00-5:07).
A great teacher "isn't a teacher": This means a great teacher facilitates enduring understanding rather than just imparting facts in a classroom setting (5:45-5:58). He uses the analogy of learning to ride a bike—it's learned by doing, not by studying its parts in a classroom (6:16-7:27).
Great teachers understand students' lives outside school: He shares the story of a student named Evette, whose academic struggles were due to her home life, not a lack of effort. He emphasizes that great teachers notice struggles, don't make assumptions, and offer support when students are stuck (8:05-10:27).
Great teachers "sing": This surprising answer means teachers are humble and willing to take risks and put aside their fear to try new things, even if it means being vulnerable (10:51, 12:42).
Terronez concludes by stressing the critical importance of listening (13:25). He points out that while reading and writing are extensively taught, formal listening instruction is almost non-existent (13:30-13:50), despite listening being a number one essential skill for business and life (14:19). He believes that if educators truly listened to students, schools and education could be transformed (14:50-14:53).

## biggest myth of education
This video debunks the popular concept of learning styles, arguing that the idea of individuals having a preferred way of learning (such as visual, auditory, reading/writing, or kinesthetic - VARK) is a stubborn myth (0:26).

Key points from the video:

The Myth of Learning Styles (0:24-0:35): The video explains that the common belief is that people learn better if information is presented in accordance with their specific learning style.
VARK Model (0:36-1:23): The VARK model, which categorizes learners as visual, auditory, reading/writing, or kinesthetic, is the most common learning style theory. However, the video asserts there's no scientific basis for students naturally clustering into these distinct groups (8:19-8:29).
Lack of Evidence (3:26-7:10): Rigorous studies, including randomized control trials, have shown no significant improvement in learning outcomes when instruction matches a student's self-proclaimed learning style (6:57-7:03). Students often do not even stick to their preferred styles in practice (7:36-7:42).
Why the Myth Persists (10:16-10:56): People often find learning styles convincing because they already believe it to be true, and they interpret experiences to fit this belief. A great diagram or explanation helps anyone learn, not just a specific "visual learner" (10:44-10:47).
Effective Learning Strategies (11:02-11:26): The video highlights that multimodal approaches, where words and pictures are presented together, are more effective for everyone. This is known as the multimedia effect, explaining why videos can be powerful learning tools when narration complements visuals.
Active Engagement is Key (11:48-12:02): The most important factor for learning is what happens inside the learner's head – active thinking, problem-solving, and imagining different scenarios lead to better learning.
Consequences of the Myth (12:19-12:38): Believing in learning styles can be detrimental, leading teachers to unnecessary worries and students to reluctance in engaging with certain instruction types, ultimately wasting time and resources that could be spent on evidence-based methods.
Critical Thinking and Google Search (13:06-14:20): The video, sponsored by Google Search, encourages viewers to think critically about information. It suggests trying different search queries, including terms like "debunked" or "false," to find diverse perspectives and assess the reliability of sources.
